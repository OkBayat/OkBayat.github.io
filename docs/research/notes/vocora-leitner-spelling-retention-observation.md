# Knowing the Spelling Today, Remembering It Weeks Later: An Early Observation from Vocora
{: .no_toc }

{ A first-person field note on daily dictation, Leitner scheduling, and delayed spelling recall | fs-6 }

{: .note-title }
> **Early observation — not a controlled scientific finding**
>
> I am recording an informal, first-person comparison that I carried out while learning English spelling with [Vocora](/work/projects/vocora). I divided a larger pool of words into two roughly equal sets using random assignment. Both sets were practiced through auditory dictation in Vocora: I heard a word and wrote its spelling. One set followed my own daily-review routine until I could spell every item correctly, which took about **one week**. The other followed Vocora's **Leitner-style review schedule** until its items reached the system's mastered state, which took about **45 days**. In a later spelling check, which I remember as being after approximately **three weeks without review**, I recalled roughly **60 out of 100** spellings from the self-managed set and **94 out of 100** from the Leitner-managed set. These are **self-reported per-100 figures** from a retrospective account. Exact denominators, item-level results, review logs, and the precise alignment of final-test dates have not been provided for independent analysis. The difference is a reason to investigate, **not proof that Leitner scheduling caused it**.

I had a simple question: when I can write every word correctly today, how much of that spelling knowledge will still be available weeks later?

I had been using a Leitner-style system in Vocora and noticed something subjectively striking about its timing. Sometimes a word would return when recall was starting to feel difficult. The act of retrieving its spelling seemed more meaningful than another easy repetition.

Rather than rely only on that impression, I tried a personal comparison: one list reviewed every day under my own stopping rule, and another list allowed to progress through the Leitner schedule.

The numbers I remember from the delayed check were substantially different. What interests me is **why**, and what a better-designed study would need to establish before treating this experience as evidence of an effect.

## Why I tried comparing the two routines

My interest was not just whether I could spell a word correctly while studying. I wanted to distinguish the feeling of having finished a word list from being able to retrieve those same spellings after a meaningful delay.

In ordinary practice, getting every answer right can feel like completion. But that result answers a short-term performance question: *Can I produce the spelling now, under these conditions?* It does not by itself answer a retention question: *Will I still be able to produce it several weeks later?*

While using Leitner-style review, I felt that an item would sometimes reappear at a moment when I had to work harder to recall it. That experience led me to wonder whether **the timing of successful retrieval** might matter more than simply repeating a word until it feels familiar.

I did not measure a forgetting threshold or observe a brain process. "Just as I was about to forget" is my impression of retrieval difficulty, **not an established property of the algorithm or a demonstrated mechanism**.

## How I organized my informal comparison

I began with a pool of **hundreds of vocabulary words**. To reduce the chance that I would knowingly place easier words in one condition, I put them into an array and randomly divided that array into two sets.

This was **word-level allocation within my own learning**, not random assignment of participants to an experiment. It is a useful attempt to avoid deliberate selection bias; it does not guarantee that the sets were equally difficult, equally familiar, or matched in word length and spelling complexity.

Both sets were practiced in Vocora. The task was deliberately narrow: listen to a word's audio, then write its spelling. My observation concerns **spelling from audio**, not whether I remembered the word's meaning, recognized it in reading, used it in conversation, or understood it in context.

### Routine A: daily review without Leitner progression

For one set, I managed the repetitions myself.

- Each day, I listened to the words and wrote their spellings.
- I continued the daily routine for approximately **one week**, until I could write all the target words correctly under my practice conditions.
- Once I had reached that all-correct point, **I stopped reviewing that set**.

My stopping rule mattered. I treated an all-correct practice result as the end of training, even though I had not checked what would remain after a longer delay.

### Routine B: review under Leitner progression

For the other set, I used the Leitner-style progression within Vocora.

- I practiced the same general kind of audio-to-spelling task.
- Review opportunities followed the application's scheduling and progression rules rather than my fixed daily review of the whole list.
- It took me approximately **45 days** for these hundreds of words to reach the system's mastered state.

The longer calendar duration was part of what I observed, **not something I held equal across conditions**. I have not supplied an item-level export of the actual intervals, number of retrieval attempts, minutes practiced, errors, or feedback exposures.

A detail that is easy to miss: **Routine A also involved spacing across days.** It was not literally "spaced practice versus no spaced practice." The comparison was between **daily review until the first all-correct stopping point** and **Leitner-scheduled review until system mastery**, with different stopping criteria and different durations.

## What happened at the delayed spelling check

After finishing the practice routines, I returned to the words to see how much spelling I could still retrieve. I remember leaving approximately **three weeks (about 21 days)** before the later check.

The exact item-level test dates, whether both sets had identical delays measured from their respective completion dates, and the final-test conditions are not documented in this account. Those details are important if the comparison is to be analyzed formally.

The outcome I remember was:

| Practice condition | Approximate correct spellings at the later check | Approximate spelling errors |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Self-managed daily review, stopped after about one week | **60 per 100** | **40 per 100** |
| Leitner-style progression, completed after about 45 days | **94 per 100** | **6 per 100** |

These are the results **as I reported them**, expressed per 100 words. They should not be read as a verified dataset with exactly 100 tested items in each group, nor as percentages calculated here from a supplied raw export.

The reported difference is **34 percentage points**. That describes the contrast in my account; it is not a causal effect estimate, an improvement in overall English proficiency, or proof that a specific review interval is optimal.

The experience surprised me because the set I had finished quickly had felt fully learned at the time. The set that took much longer to finish appeared to remain more accessible when I returned to its spellings.

I was not testing word meanings, pronunciation quality, comprehension, or translation. **This observation is strictly about my later ability to write spellings after hearing the words.**

## Observation, interpretation, and hypothesis are different things

I want to keep three layers separate.

### What I directly experienced

I followed two different practice routines, reached their respective completion points, and later found that far more spellings seemed available from the Leitner-managed set. The delayed check was a practical attempt to see what remained after a pause rather than another study session.

### What I suspect, but have not demonstrated

My working interpretation is that **repeated retrieval after longer or changing intervals** may have strengthened later access to those spellings. When recall required some effort but was still successful, the repetition felt more consequential than an easy same-day repetition.

That is a hypothesis about a possible mechanism. I did not quantify retrieval difficulty, determine when an item was "almost forgotten," or compare equal numbers of retrieval attempts across the two conditions.

### What this comparison cannot establish

The observation cannot isolate the benefit of spacing, Leitner progression, retrieval effort, total exposure, or simply **continuing training much longer**.

Both routines included retrieval. Both included some spacing. They differed in when words were revisited, when training stopped, and how long the entire process lasted. The later result could reflect several of those differences together.

## Why the comparison is suggestive but not a controlled trial

Even though I attempted to randomize the words, several limitations matter:

1. **One learner, one informal comparison.** The only participant was me. There are no independent learner groups or blinded assessors.
2. **Unknown exact set sizes.** I have reported hundreds of words and per-100 outcomes, but have not provided the exact item counts or a reproducible item-level score sheet.
3. **Different completion rules.** In the daily condition, I stopped after reaching all-correct performance. In the Leitner condition, I continued until the program marked items mastered. These are not equivalent criteria.
4. **Different total training periods.** Approximately seven days versus 45 days is a substantial difference. Total retrievals and time spent were not matched.
5. **Daily practice was already distributed.** Calling the daily group "massed practice" would be misleading. The real comparison concerns scheduling pattern, duration, and stopping behavior.
6. **No independently verified baseline.** Random assignment might reduce deliberate selection bias, but without a spelling pretest, familiarity and difficulty could still differ between sets by chance.
7. **Delayed-test timing is approximate.** The remembered three-week pause needs to be anchored to each group's actual final practice date.
8. **Assessment details are incomplete.** The prompt order, whether there were hints or replays, exact-match scoring rules, treatment of typos, and possible incidental exposure have not been documented.
9. **My roles were not independent.** I was the learner, the designer of the comparison, and the person evaluating my outcome. Expectations and recording errors could influence the account.

For these reasons, I would not describe this as a randomized controlled study or claim that Vocora produced a scientifically established **94% retention rate**. It is an early observation with an informal randomized allocation of words, an apparent difference in a delayed check, and several plausible explanations.

## What published research helps me ask

My experience did not reveal a new law of memory. There is already substantial research on distributed practice and retrieval.

A quantitative synthesis by **Cepeda and colleagues (2006)** examined how intervals between learning episodes relate to later retention. A later review by **Kang (2016)** described the value of distributing encounters over time and combining spacing with retrieval. More specifically for languages, **Kim and Webb (2022)** meta-analyzed spaced practice in second-language learning, finding effects that depend in part on the task, interval, and assessment timing.

Research by **Karpicke and Roediger (2008)** also draws attention to an idea closely related to my stopping rule: **being able to answer correctly is not necessarily the same as having no further benefit from retrieval practice**.

These findings support investigating scheduling, delayed retention, and continued retrieval. **None of these papers studied this personal comparison, validated the exact Vocora Leitner rules, or established the mechanism behind the numbers I reported.**

An additional nuance from the literature matters: a longer interval is not invariably better, and an expanding schedule has not been demonstrated to be uniquely optimal for every item, learner, or retention goal. The interesting question is not whether "more difficulty" is always good, but **under what conditions retrieval remains successful and useful over time**.

## How I would test this more carefully

A future study could preserve the useful part of my attempt—allocating comparable words to different routines—while making the comparisons fairer and the outcomes independently inspectable.

1. **Record the full word inventory and allocation.** Keep word IDs, initial familiarity, length, spelling difficulty, assignment seed, and exclusion criteria.
2. **Pretest spelling from audio.** Establish whether words were already known before training, and stratify assignment by baseline difficulty where possible.
3. **Specify the learning conditions.** Include a daily review-until-criterion condition, a Leitner-scheduled condition, and ideally a condition that matches total retrieval attempts or study time.
4. **Log every learning event.** Preserve timestamps, correct and incorrect attempts, replay and hint use, feedback, interval, time on task, and each word's mastery date.
5. **Use identical test conditions.** Play the audio under the same rules, collect the spelling without corrective feedback, shuffle items, and score against predefined spelling criteria.
6. **Fix the retention intervals.** Compare the groups at the same delays after their last relevant practice—for example, 21 days and later follow-ups—rather than only on one calendar date.
7. **Separate different questions.** Test spelling from audio first; any claim about meaning, productive vocabulary, or real-world language use would require different assessments.
8. **Recruit additional learners and blind the scoring.** Avoid treating an encouraging experience from a single learner as a general product outcome.

With appropriate controls, such a study could ask whether the **schedule itself** matters beyond the total number of successful retrievals, time spent, or the decision to stop reviewing once a word can be written correctly.

## How this relates to my other Vocora observations

This note is about **delayed spelling retention under two practice schedules**. It should not be merged with my other exploratory observations, which concern different outcomes:

- [From Knowing Grammar to Using It Naturally](/research-practice/notes/vocora-grammar-micro-skills-speaking-transfer-observation) describes perceived changes in spontaneous grammar use and a later experience of film-dialogue comprehension after micro-skill practice.
- [When Less Attention to Spelling Seems to Help](/research-practice/notes/vocora-dictation-attention-observation) concerns a subjective impression that consciously monitoring spelling less sometimes coincided with more accurate dictation.
- [From Rules to Chunks](/research-practice/notes/vocora-grammar-automaticity-observation) describes noticing grammatical patterns and assembling shuffled sentences through chunks.

The present comparison involved a self-reported delayed spelling tally, whereas the earlier dictation note did **not** include a comparable measured attention-versus-accuracy experiment.

## The question I want to investigate

**For English spelling learned through audio dictation, does continuing successful retrieval on a Leitner-style schedule lead to better delayed spelling recall than stopping a daily-review routine as soon as all words are correct—and, if a difference appears, how much is attributable to review timing rather than total practice, stopping criteria, prior word familiarity, or the delay before testing?**
