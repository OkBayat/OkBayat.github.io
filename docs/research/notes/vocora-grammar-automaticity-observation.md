# From Rules to Chunks: An Early Observation of Automatic Grammar Processing in Vocora
{: .no_toc }

{ A first-person learning observation after approximately six weeks of practice | fs-6 }

{: .note-title }
> **Early observation — not a research finding**
>
> This is a first-person account of a change I noticed while learning English with [Vocora](/work/projects/vocora), a learning system I am also building. I am both its developer and the learner reporting the experience. The account is retrospective, informal, and limited to one person. It is not a controlled experiment, a validated measure of implicit learning, or evidence that Vocora improves speaking fluency. My examples below illustrate the patterns I noticed; they are not presented as verbatim exercise logs or timed observations.

Something changed in the way I answer grammar exercises, and the change surprised me.

For roughly six weeks, I had been practicing English grammar in Vocora. Grammar was organized into skills and smaller, specific **micro-skills**. I worked through exercises and returned to material through a Leitner-style spaced-review routine. Early in that period, even simple activities that asked me to arrange shuffled words into a sentence could leave me confused. I often had to stop, inspect the options, identify the grammatical rule, and work out where each word belonged. Sometimes I lost track of the sentence altogether.

Recently, I noticed two different behaviors. With familiar patterns, I sometimes anticipate what kind of word should come next before consciously checking all the remaining words. And when a sentence contains many words, I often no longer try to build it from the first word to the last. Instead, I recognize small grammatical groups in different places, assemble those pieces, and join them into a complete answer.

The experience feels fast and largely unplanned. I do not necessarily think, “This is present perfect,” or “This is the second conditional,” before acting. I see part of a structure, and a possible next move seems to become available.

That is the observation. **Whether it reflects automatization, prediction, chunk learning, task familiarity, or some combination remains an open question.**

## The learning setting: micro-skills, review, and shuffled words

The relevant Vocora exercises present a question or context and ask the learner to form an answer by selecting words displayed in a shuffled order. The words are visible, but their correct arrangement is not. Completing the task requires attending to the question and arranging the available words into a grammatical response.

I had practiced grammar as smaller units rather than treating an entire tense or construction as one undifferentiated ability. For example, recognizing an auxiliary, choosing an appropriate verb form, and connecting the parts of a conditional sentence can be considered distinct targets for learning and practice. I also revisited material over time through Leitner-style review.

I am describing my experience with that practice arrangement, not claiming that each element independently produced the change. I did not isolate the effects of micro-skill decomposition, repeated retrieval, spacing, the question prompt, or familiarity with the interface. Nor did I keep a predeclared record of practice volume or response times. “Approximately six weeks” is the duration of the experience, **not** a measured dose–response relationship.

## Before: building a sentence by conscious inspection

My earlier approach was slow and heavily deliberate.

When an exercise appeared, I would first read the question, then look over the shuffled words, and try to identify the grammatical pattern. I might ask myself which tense was needed, where an auxiliary should go, or which verb form belonged after it. I attempted to order the words from the beginning of the sentence toward the end.

This was not just a matter of occasionally making a grammatical error. Sometimes the number of choices was enough to make me lose the structure entirely. I could know that a sentence was relatively simple and still struggle to see how its pieces connected.

A simplified representation of that earlier approach is:

1. Read the question and inspect the word options.
2. Try to name or reason through the grammar rule.
3. Find a plausible first word.
4. Continue word by word, checking and correcting the order.
5. Reconsider the whole sentence when something does not fit.

I am not saying I *always* used precisely these five steps. This is a reconstruction of the conscious, sequential effort I remember.

## Observation one: anticipating the next part of a grammatical pattern

The first change concerns **anticipation**.

Sometimes, when I see a small part of a possible answer, it immediately suggests what kind of words could follow. For example, in the right question context, seeing **“I”** and **“have”** together may lead me toward **“I have never ...”** and then toward a past-participle verb form. Rather than deliberately recalling the present-perfect rule, I begin searching for a word that fits the emerging pattern.

Consider an *illustrative* exercise:

**Question:** Have you ever visited London?

**Shuffled words:** visited · never · London · I · have

**Answer:** I have never visited London.

In the way I now experience some exercises, recognizing **“I have”** can rapidly activate the possibility of **“never”** and a participle such as **“visited.”** I may begin arranging the words before giving each option the same conscious attention I used to give it. The question itself matters: it constrains what a plausible response looks like.

This is not a claim that **“I have”** always predicts **“never”**, or even a past participle. The same two words can begin a different construction, such as **“I have a book.”** What I am noticing is a context-sensitive expectation: a few cues seem to make particular grammatical continuations more available than others.

The closest everyday analogy I can think of is predictive text on a smartphone keyboard. After one or two words, the keyboard proposes plausible next words. I do not believe the brain literally runs the same algorithm. The analogy simply describes the *felt experience* of rapidly narrowing down the likely next pieces of a sentence.

The important difference from my earlier experience is not that I can recite a rule more quickly. It is that, at least on some practiced exercises, **I do not notice myself reciting the rule at all**.

## Observation two: assembling long sentences non-linearly

The second change is more distinctive to me: **I no longer necessarily solve long shuffled sentences from left to right**.

When many word tiles are visible, I often recognize several small grammatical patterns independently. I may complete a piece near the end of the sentence, then identify a piece at the beginning, then build another piece in the middle. After that, I connect what remains.

A second conditional sentence provides a useful illustration.

**Question:** What would you do if you had more time?

**Shuffled words:** practice · time · would · more · every · if · I · day · had · I

**Possible answer:** If I had more time, I would practice every day.

The conventional *linear* route is to begin with **“If”**, then choose **“I”**, then **“had”**, and continue until the whole sentence is complete:

1. **If I had more time**
2. **I would practice**
3. **every day**

But the assembly process I now notice can be *non-linear*. For example, **“would”** may immediately make me look for a base-form verb, such as **“practice.”** I recognize **“would practice”** as a local pattern even when it is not the first part of the answer. Separately, I may locate the conditional clause **“if I had more time”**. I can also recognize **“every day”** as a meaningful phrase. I then connect the pieces into the final sentence:

- **Middle anchor first:** “would” + base verb → **“would practice”**
- **Conditional clause:** “if” + subject + past-form verb → **“if I had more time”**
- **Remaining phrase:** **“every day”**
- **Final integration:** **“If I had more time, I would practice every day.”**

This is a schematic reconstruction of how recognition can guide the work; it is not a record of the exact sequence in which I tapped individual tiles on a particular occasion. The answer still has to be grammatical and appropriate to the question. I may revise a chunk when another one shows it cannot fit.

The main observation is about **where construction starts**. Previously, I often needed to secure the beginning of the sentence before I could do anything useful. Now a cue in the middle or near the end can give me a starting point. The sentence feels less like a single chain of isolated decisions and more like a puzzle made of recognizable parts.

## What seems to have changed

I can describe the subjective contrast, even though I cannot yet quantify it:

| Earlier experience | Recent experience |
| --- | --- |
| Many shuffled words could feel confusing, even in relatively simple sentences. | Familiar word patterns often stand out quickly. |
| I often tried to identify the name of a grammar rule first. | I can sometimes act without consciously naming the rule. |
| I generally attempted to build the answer in order, from the start. | I may identify middle, beginning, and ending chunks in a different order. |
| I evaluated many individual tiles separately. | Some tiles appear to invite a nearby grammatical partner or group. |
| When I became stuck, I could lose the structure of the whole sentence. | Recognized local structures can provide several points from which to reconstruct the whole. |

I also have a subjective impression of much faster completion. **I do not have reliable before-and-after timings, an independently scored accuracy series, or a controlled comparison**, so I cannot report a speedup or an improvement percentage. The change is meaningful to me as a learner, but its size and generality are not established.

## How this observation might be interpreted

Three research concepts may help turn the experience into questions. They are **interpretations**, not diagnoses of the underlying mental process.

### 1. Automatization of practiced grammatical operations

Second-language skill-acquisition research has examined how performance following explicit rule learning can become more efficient with practice, with important limits on transfer across tasks. In one classic study, DeKeyser investigated the development of second-language morphosyntactic skills after explicit rule learning.

My experience is consistent with the *possibility* that some familiar operations now require less deliberate step-by-step attention. But that does not establish that an explicit rule has become implicit knowledge. Less awareness of a rule while answering is not, by itself, a valid test of implicit learning.

### 2. Predictive use of context and familiar structures

The first observation feels predictive: a question and an initial word pattern seem to constrain what I search for next. This could involve expectations formed from repeated encounters with familiar constructions.

The key distinction is between **my experience of anticipation** and an objectively demonstrated prediction mechanism. An experiment would need to test whether recognizing partial structures changes what I expect or how quickly I identify valid continuations, including novel sentences.

### 3. Chunking and pattern recognition

The second observation fits the everyday description of *chunking*: treating several elements as a familiar, meaningful unit rather than processing every element as independent. Work on chunking in learning and theories of second-language constructions provides relevant conceptual background.

In my case, phrases such as **“would practice”** or **“if I had more time”** may become useful subassemblies. They do not have to be completed in spoken or written order while I am solving a word-order puzzle. That is different from claiming that language itself is produced in arbitrary order; the **internal assembly strategy** may be non-linear while the final English sentence remains linear.

## Other explanations I need to take seriously

Several plausible explanations compete with, or could contribute to, automatization and chunk recognition:

- **Task familiarity:** I may have learned to operate Vocora's shuffled-word interface more efficiently.
- **Repeated items or templates:** I may recognize recurring sentence forms, rather than generalizing to genuinely new constructions.
- **Prompt support:** The question and the visible word choices narrow the answer space considerably.
- **Recognition rather than spontaneous production:** Selecting supplied words is easier in important ways than producing all the words independently.
- **Attention and expectation:** After noticing improvement, I may be more attentive to successful fast responses than to continued errors.
- **Unmeasured accuracy:** Speed may rise because of risk-taking or shortcuts; without response-level data, I cannot separate efficient processing from speed–accuracy tradeoffs.

These are not minor objections to be dismissed. They define what a later investigation would need to distinguish. In particular, **a smoother word-order exercise does not demonstrate greater conversational fluency**, although the possibility of transfer to speaking is worth testing.

## From a personal observation to a testable question

If I were to examine this phenomenon beyond my own experience, I would start by measuring behavior rather than assuming a hidden learning mechanism. A future study could compare performance before and after a defined period of micro-skill practice with spaced review, while keeping the sentence materials and testing conditions explicit.

Useful measurements would include:

- **Response time and accuracy**, reported together, on both practiced and unseen sentence patterns.
- **Tile-selection order**, to test whether learners actually assemble recognizable chunks from several positions rather than always proceeding left to right.
- **Response to partial cues**, to examine whether a small number of words helps identify a correct continuation.
- **Transfer tasks**, such as completing a sentence without supplied word tiles, constructing a new sentence from a prompt, and speaking in response to a question.
- **Delayed performance**, so improvement within a practice session is not mistaken for retention.
- **Comparison conditions**, separating the effects of micro-skill practice, spacing, exposure, and interface familiarity where the design permits.

Such a study would require prospectively defined outcomes, appropriate consent and privacy protection for learner interaction data, and careful analysis of alternative explanations. My personal account supplies a hypothesis to test, not a result to generalize.

### Relevant research background

The following publications help frame possible mechanisms; **none evaluated this individual observation or demonstrated Vocora's effectiveness**.

- DeKeyser, R. M. (1997). [Beyond Explicit Rule Learning: Automatizing Second Language Morphosyntax](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263197002040). *Studies in Second Language Acquisition*. Related to practice and development of second-language grammatical skills.
- Gobet, F., et al. (2001). [Chunking Mechanisms in Human Learning](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(00)01662-4). *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*. A broader review of grouping elements into meaningful units.
- Ellis, N. C. (2003). [Constructions, Chunking, and Connectionism: The Emergence of Second Language Structure](https://doi.org/10.1002/9780470756492.ch4). In *The Handbook of Second Language Acquisition*. A theoretical perspective on chunks and learned language structures.
- Kim, Y., & Webb, S. (2022). [The Effects of Spaced Practice on Second Language Learning: A Meta-Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479). *Language Learning*. Context for spaced language practice, not evidence about this particular task or schedule.

## The question I want to investigate

**Can repeated practice of targeted grammar micro-skills, combined with spaced review, help learners move from conscious, word-by-word rule application toward rapid grammatical prediction and non-linear, chunk-based sentence assembly—and, if so, does that change transfer beyond shuffled-word exercises to independent sentence production and spontaneous speaking?**
