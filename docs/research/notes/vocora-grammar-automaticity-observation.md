# From Rules to Chunks: An Early Observation of Grammar Practice in Vocora
{: .no_toc }

{ A first-person learning observation after approximately six weeks of practice | fs-6 }

{: .note-title }
> **Early observation · one learner**
>
> I am both the developer of [Vocora](/work/projects/vocora) and the learner reporting this experience. This retrospective account describes a change in how I approached grammar exercises. It includes no timed comparison or independent assessment.

After roughly **six weeks** of grammar practice, I noticed that I was approaching shuffled-word exercises differently. Familiar patterns sometimes suggested the next part of a sentence before I consciously recalled a rule. In longer sentences, I could often recognize several small groups of words and connect them, instead of working strictly from the first word to the last.

The experience felt faster and less effortful. The most distinctive change was having more than one place to start: a useful pattern near the middle or end could help me work out the rest.

## The practice setting

The exercises presented a question or context and a set of shuffled words. I had to select the words in an order that formed an appropriate response. The words were supplied; I was not producing a sentence entirely from memory.

I practiced grammar through skills and smaller **micro-skills**, such as selecting a verb form or connecting parts of a conditional sentence, and returned to material through a Leitner-style review routine. The six-week duration is an approximate recollection. I did not prospectively record practice volume or isolate the contributions of micro-skills, spacing, repetition, and interface familiarity.

Early in this period, I often inspected the words, tried to identify the grammatical rule, and built the answer from left to right. With many options, I could lose track of the structure even when the underlying sentence was fairly simple.

## Recognizing a possible continuation

The first change was an impression of anticipation. A question and a few words could make a plausible continuation stand out.

For example, consider this **illustrative exercise**, rather than a recorded item from a practice session:

**Question:** Have you ever visited London?

**Shuffled words:** visited · never · London · I · have

**Answer:** I have never visited London.

In this context, recognizing **“I have”** can lead me toward **“never”** and a participle such as **“visited.”** I sometimes begin arranging the words without first thinking, “This is the present perfect.” The question and available words help narrow the possibilities.

The context matters. **“I have”** could also begin **“I have a book.”** My observation is about a likely continuation within a particular exercise, not an automatic rule that these words always predict one construction.

Predictive text is a useful analogy for how this feels: a small cue makes some next steps more available. It does not identify the mechanism involved in my own learning.

## Building a sentence from several starting points

The second change concerned longer sentences. I began noticing local patterns in different positions and connecting them into a complete answer.

A second conditional illustrates the experience:

**Question:** What would you do if you had more time?

**Shuffled words:** practice · time · would · more · every · if · I · day · had · I

**Possible answer:** If I had more time, I would practice every day.

I might first notice **“would practice,”** then recognize **“if I had more time,”** and then place **“every day.”** These are possible starting points, not a logged sequence of taps. I still need to connect the pieces correctly and check that the whole sentence answers the question.

Previously, I often felt unable to proceed until I had secured the beginning of the sentence. Now a recognizable group elsewhere could give me a way in. The final sentence still has a conventional word order; what changed was the order in which I seemed to work on its parts.

Both examples reconstruct the kind of pattern I noticed. They are not transcripts or evidence that a specific sequence occurred at a measured speed.

## What I can conclude from the experience

I can report three perceived changes: familiar continuations stood out more readily, I relied less on consciously naming rules, and I could assemble some sentences through recognizable parts. I also felt faster, but I have no reliable before-and-after timings or independently assessed accuracy series. A numerical speedup would go beyond the evidence.

Research offers several ways to frame the observation. [DeKeyser's study of morphosyntactic practice](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263197002040) examined the development of grammatical skills after explicit learning and found that improvement was strongly specific to the practiced skill. That makes it relevant to this account while also leaving transfer to other tasks unresolved. [Gobet and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(00)01662-4) distinguish deliberate and more automatic forms of chunking; recognizing groups of words does not by itself tell me which process occurred. [Ellis's account of constructions and chunking](https://doi.org/10.1002/9780470756492.ch4) provides further conceptual background for learning familiar language patterns.

These are possible interpretations of my report. Less conscious rule naming is not sufficient to establish implicit grammatical knowledge. Repeated items, familiar templates, the question prompt, visible word choices, and growing ease with the interface could all contribute to the experience. Without jointly measuring speed and accuracy, I also cannot tell whether every faster response was an improvement.

The wider evidence on [spaced second-language practice](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479) supports investigating scheduling, but it does not validate my particular routine or separate spacing from the other parts of my practice.

## How this fits the other observations

My [note on grammar use in speaking and listening](/research-practice/notes/vocora-grammar-micro-skills-speaking-transfer-observation) concerns different outcomes: a speaking lesson and my experience of film dialogue. This note concerns solving exercises with supplied words.

Both belong to my ongoing experience with Vocora. Their practice periods may overlap, and the approximate durations are not independently dated intervention windows. They should be read as related observations from the same learner, rather than counted as separate studies demonstrating an effect.

## A focused next question

The first useful test would record **accuracy, response time, and tile-selection order** on both practiced and unfamiliar sentences. This could establish whether the reported change in assembly actually occurs and whether it extends beyond repeated items. Sentence production without supplied words, followed by a delayed assessment, would address a further question about transfer and retention.

**Does practice make familiar grammatical patterns easier to recognize and combine, and how much of that change carries over when the words are no longer supplied?**
