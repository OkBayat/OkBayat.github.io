# When Less Spelling Monitoring Seems to Help: An Early Observation from Vocora Dictation
{: .no_toc }

{ A first-person note about where attention goes while answering | fs-6 }

{: .note-title }
> **Early observation · one learner**
>
> I am both the learner reporting this experience and the developer of [Vocora](/work/projects/vocora). This retrospective account records an impression about spelling accuracy. I have not compared measured accuracy under controlled attention conditions.

During some dictation exercises, I noticed something that surprised me: my answers seemed more accurate when I stopped consciously working through every spelling decision. When I recognized what I heard and entered the answer more directly, the response sometimes felt easier and more successful.

This happened during a broader period of approximately a month and a half of language practice, including grammar micro-skills and Leitner-style review. I cannot identify an exact onset. The timing connects the observation to my learning experience; it does not identify which part of the practice, if any, produced it.

The question is whether the accuracy impression survives measurement, and what would explain it if it does.

## What I noticed while answering

The relevant activities involved hearing English and entering its written form. I sometimes focused closely on deciding and checking how each word should be spelled. At other times, I recognized the spoken words and entered their spellings with less deliberate scrutiny of individual letters. In the second situation, I had the impression that more of my answers were correct.

By **less spelling monitoring**, I mean less conscious checking of each spelling decision while forming the response. I do not mean that I stopped listening, ignored the task, or deliberately accepted mistakes. Attention could still be directed toward the audio, the phrase, or the answer as a whole.

I did not record these as separate experimental conditions. I have no matched accuracy figures for moments of greater and lesser monitoring, and no reliable timing comparison. A response feeling smooth is part of the experience I noticed; it is not itself a measure of correctness.

This distinction matters because the relationship could run in either direction. Reduced monitoring might make a familiar response easier to produce. Equally, an easy or well-known word might require less monitoring in the first place.

## What research helps me ask

Three research perspectives help frame the question, with different limits on their relevance.

**Access to familiar responses.** Logan's [instance theory of automatization (1988)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.95.4.492) describes how consistent practice can support retrieval of stored instances. It offers one possible way to think about familiar written forms becoming more readily available. My subjective experience does not establish that this mechanism explains my dictation performance, or that my spelling knowledge has become implicit.

**Monitoring a practiced action.** [Beilock and Carr (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.130.4.701) studied performance under pressure in golf putting and alphabet arithmetic. Their findings supported an explicit-monitoring account for skilled putting, with different results in alphabet arithmetic. This makes the task boundary important: the study gives a reason to ask about step-by-step monitoring, but it does not show that monitoring spelling harms dictation. I also did not manipulate performance pressure in my own practice.

**Attention in learning.** [Schmidt (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/11.2.129) distinguished questions about noticing, incidental learning, and implicit learning. That discussion concerns how language is learned, whereas my immediate observation concerns how it feels to spell familiar words during dictation. It would be a mistake to turn my account into advice to pay less attention when learning an unfamiliar spelling or reviewing feedback.

These sources provide questions and distinctions. None tested this observation in Vocora.

## The alternatives that matter most

The first explanation to examine is **item difficulty**. If I consciously check unfamiliar words and respond directly to familiar ones, the apparent relationship between monitoring and accuracy could arise without any benefit from reducing monitoring.

Other factors could also contribute:

- **Repeated exposure:** Some words or phrases may have become easier through previous encounters.
- **Listening and memory demands:** The length or clarity of an audio item may change how much effort is available for spelling.
- **Selective recall:** An unexpectedly successful response without much conscious checking may be more memorable than an unsuccessful one.
- **Changes across sessions:** Fatigue, familiarity with the interface, or general language practice could affect both perceived effort and accuracy.

These possibilities cannot be separated by recollection alone. Even an immediate accuracy advantage would leave a further question: does the same approach support delayed spelling retention?

## How this fits with my other observations

My [grammar assembly note](/research-practice/notes/vocora-grammar-automaticity-observation) concerns recognizing patterns in visible word tiles. This note concerns spelling in response to audio. Both arose during the same broad practice period, but they describe different tasks and are not independent demonstrations of a common mechanism.

There is also an apparent tension with my [reflection note](/research-practice/notes/vocora-metacognitive-reflection-observation), where I reported becoming more aware of rushing through familiar exercises after introducing brief reflection prompts. The two accounts concern different moments and targets of attention: pausing to reconsider a learning habit differs from consciously checking every letter while producing an answer. That is a useful distinction to test, not a demonstrated explanation that resolves the two observations.

The [delayed spelling comparison](/research-practice/notes/vocora-leitner-spelling-retention-observation) reports a later spelling check under two review routines. Its figures do not measure the attention contrast described here and should not be used as evidence for it.

## What I would measure next

A prospective study could test the effects of explicit attention instructions. These instructions would differ from the spontaneous changes in monitoring described here. It could ask a learner to monitor spelling explicitly on some trials and to focus on the spoken message while responding naturally on others. Familiarity, item length, audio conditions, and order would need to be addressed so that difficult words were not systematically assigned to one condition.

The main outcomes would be spelling accuracy, error types, and response time considered together. A brief self-report could record the learner's experienced focus, without treating it as a direct measure of unconscious processing. Delayed checks would address retention separately from immediate performance.

**Does reducing deliberate spelling monitoring change measured dictation accuracy when item difficulty and familiarity are comparable?** That is the question this observation leaves open.
