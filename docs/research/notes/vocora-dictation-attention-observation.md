# When Less Attention to Spelling Seems to Help: An Early Observation from Vocora Dictation
{: .no_toc }

{ A first-person note about attention, dictation, and the experience of accuracy | fs-6 }

{: .note-title }
> **Early observation — not an established finding**
>
> I am documenting a change I have noticed while learning with [Vocora](/work/projects/vocora), a system I am also building. This is my own informal, retrospective observation, not a completed experiment. In some dictation or spelling-related exercises, I have the impression that my responses are more accurate **when I am not consciously concentrating on spelling each word**. I have not yet tested that impression against response-level records, equivalent exercises, or a controlled comparison. The examples in this note are illustrative, not verbatim exercise logs. I am recording a question, not claiming an effect or identifying a proven cognitive mechanism.

I had assumed that carefully monitoring spelling should help me avoid mistakes. More attention to individual letters should, in principle, mean fewer missed or misplaced letters. But during recent practice, I began noticing something that did not fit that expectation.

In some dictation-style activities, **when I stop actively thinking about how to spell each word, I seem to answer more accurately**. Conversely, when I deliberately concentrate on spelling as a sequence of decisions, the task does not always feel easier or more successful.

I noticed this while using Vocora over the broader period of approximately a month and a half of language practice, including grammar micro-skills and Leitner-style review. I cannot say precisely when this feeling first appeared, nor can I attribute it to one kind of exercise. The connection to the practice period is a description of timing, **not** evidence that micro-skills, repetition, or the Leitner schedule caused this change.

I want to preserve the observation while its details are fresh, without converting an impression into a scientific conclusion.

## The setting: hearing language and entering a written answer

The activities that prompted this note involve dictation or spelling: I encounter spoken English and provide its written form. These tasks are more than a test of memorized spelling. They can involve listening, recognizing words, holding a phrase in memory, choosing written forms, entering an answer, and monitoring whether the answer matches what I heard.

Those operations may compete for attention. A learner can attend closely to the sound, the phrase as a whole, the meaning, the individual letters, or the possibility of making a mistake. The balance among these activities can shift even when the visible task looks the same.

This note is about **the focus of attention while answering**, not about proof of a specific teaching method. I did not compare identical audio items under experimentally controlled instructions, and I did not calculate accuracy percentages for “monitoring” and “not monitoring” conditions.

## The observation: less conscious spelling control, apparently better answers

The contrast I noticed is simple but surprising.

When I focus heavily on spelling, I am more consciously involved in deciding and checking how the word should be written. I may be thinking about the form of the word as something that has to be constructed correctly, letter by letter. That seems like the careful strategy.

At other times, I recognize what I hear and enter the answer without giving each spelling decision the same conscious scrutiny. The written response can feel more immediate. In those moments I have had the **impression** that my answers are more often correct.

What stands out is not that I ignore the words or stop listening. It is that **explicit attention to individual spelling choices recedes**. I may still be attending to the audio and the answer; I am simply not continually interrupting the process to evaluate the spelling of every word.

Here is a schematic illustration, **not a report of an actual item I completed**:

**Audio prompt (illustrative):** “I will finish tomorrow.”

**A consciously monitored route:** Hear the phrase → hold each word in mind → consider its letters, especially a word like “tomorrow” → type → recheck.

**A less explicitly monitored route:** Hear and recognize the phrase → enter “I will finish tomorrow” with little deliberate letter-by-letter reasoning → check the answer when appropriate.

Either route could be correct or incorrect. The illustration explains the difference in *experience* I am trying to capture. It does **not** demonstrate that the second route is objectively more accurate, faster, or more effective for learning.

The important observation is the apparent reversal of a common intuition: **more conscious control is not always experienced as better performance**.

## Why I find this different from my earlier grammar observation

In a separate note, [From Rules to Chunks: An Early Observation of Automatic Grammar Processing in Vocora](/research-practice/notes/vocora-grammar-automaticity-observation), I described recognizing grammatical patterns and assembling shuffled sentences from chunks rather than necessarily constructing them from left to right.

This dictation observation is related but distinct:

| Earlier grammar observation | Current dictation observation |
| --- | --- |
| Task: arrange visible, shuffled words to answer a question. | Task: hear language and provide a written answer. |
| What seemed to change: predicting structures and locating grammatical chunks. | What seemed to change: the amount of deliberate attention devoted to spelling each word. |
| Subjective experience: recognizable patterns guide sentence assembly. | Subjective experience: written answers can feel more accurate when letter-by-letter monitoring is reduced. |
| Main uncertainty: is this transferable structural knowledge or a task-specific strategy? | Main uncertainty: is the accuracy impression real, and what explains it if it is? |

Both accounts arose during the same broad period of practice. Neither establishes that knowledge has become “implicit.” An action that feels automatic might be supported by remembered word forms, increasing familiarity, attention to context, different item difficulty, or a mixture of these factors. I cannot separate them from introspection alone.

## Possible interpretations — not conclusions

Several ideas from cognitive psychology and second-language learning make this observation interesting. None should be mistaken for a demonstrated explanation of this individual case.

### Practice and access to familiar forms

With experience, some recurring words or letter sequences might be retrieved more readily as wholes rather than assembled through explicit, sequential decisions. Gordon Logan's account of automatization is one general theoretical model in which repeated exposure can change the basis of skilled processing.

This is a **possibility**, not a determination that my spelling knowledge has become automatic. To investigate it, we would need to look at familiar versus unfamiliar words, practiced versus unpracticed items, and speed **together with** accuracy.

### The cost of monitoring a practiced action

Research in other domains has asked whether deliberately monitoring a skilled procedure can interfere with performance. For example, Beilock and Carr investigated explicit monitoring and performance under pressure, including skilled golf putting. Their findings make the general question worth considering; they do **not** show that attending to spelling causes errors or that dictation works like golf.

I do not know whether explicit spelling monitoring interferes with my performance. It could also be that I start monitoring *because* I have already encountered a difficult or unfamiliar word. In that case the apparent relationship would run in the other direction.

### Attention during learning versus attention during performance

It is also important not to confuse two different questions: **Where should attention go while learning?** and **What should be consciously monitored when carrying out a task after practice?** Research on attention and consciousness in second-language learning has treated noticing and learning as complex issues, not as proof that attention is generally harmful.

My observation concerns how it *feels to perform* a dictation task. It is not a recommendation for learners to stop paying attention to spelling when learning unfamiliar words, reviewing feedback, or correcting errors.

## What might explain the impression without any change in spelling skill?

Before making claims, I would need to consider several alternatives:

- **Item difficulty:** Perhaps I monitor spelling most when a word is already difficult, and stop monitoring when the answer is familiar. That alone could produce the pattern.
- **Repeated exposure:** Easier responses might involve words and phrases I have encountered many times before.
- **Listening and memory load:** Spending attention on individual letters might leave less available for holding a spoken phrase in mind; alternatively, the phrase may simply be easier on some trials.
- **Response strategy:** A fast, fluent-feeling answer may be more satisfying even when it contains errors. I need actual correctness data, not just a feeling.
- **Selective recall:** I may remember striking cases when “not thinking” worked, and overlook equally frequent cases when it failed.
- **Changes over time:** Overall familiarity with English and the interface may have improved irrespective of my momentary attention strategy.
- **Ambiguity in “not paying attention”:** Reduced *spelling-specific* monitoring is not the same as distraction or total inattention. A future study would have to define and distinguish these conditions.

A successful answer in a dictation exercise also does not establish independent writing ability, durable spelling knowledge, or fluency in speaking.

## A more careful way to investigate it

The next step would be to collect data prospectively, rather than infer improvement from this account. A small pilot could begin with the following design:

1. **Comparable dictation items:** Include familiar and previously unseen words or sentences, with controlled length, spelling difficulty, and listening conditions.
2. **Defined attention conditions:** On randomly assigned trials, invite the learner either to monitor spelling explicitly or to focus on comprehending the spoken message while responding naturally. Do not interpret the latter as “ignore correctness.”
3. **Accuracy plus timing:** Record exact spelling errors, omission or substitution types, completion time, and any post-answer corrections. Accuracy must remain primary; speed alone is not improvement.
4. **Within-learner comparison:** Compare the same learner across balanced items and repeated sessions, while testing order and practice effects. Only then consider a larger sample.
5. **Learning and transfer:** Test unfamiliar items and delayed retention. A momentary performance difference is not necessarily a lasting learning benefit.
6. **Self-report separately:** Record where learners *think* their attention was directed, but do not treat self-report as a direct measurement of unconscious processing.

Any learner-level study would need permission, privacy protection, and predefined reporting rules. Until such evidence exists, I should not say that reduced attention causes higher spelling accuracy, that a training method produced it, or that all learners would benefit.

## Research background

These are **conceptual starting points**, not studies demonstrating this effect in Vocora:

- Logan, G. D. (1988). [Toward an Instance Theory of Automatization](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.95.4.492). *Psychological Review*, 95(4), 492–527. An account of how repeated practice may affect retrieval and skilled processing.
- Beilock, S. L., & Carr, T. H. (2001). [On the Fragility of Skilled Performance: What Governs Choking Under Pressure?](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.130.4.701). *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 130(4), 701–725. Studies explicit monitoring in tasks **other than dictation**; not direct evidence for spelling.
- Schmidt, R. W. (1990). [The Role of Consciousness in Second Language Learning](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/11.2.129). *Applied Linguistics*, 11(2), 129–158. A useful distinction between attention, conscious noticing, and claims about implicit learning.
- Related Vocora notes: [grammar prediction and chunk-based sentence assembly](/research-practice/notes/vocora-grammar-automaticity-observation) and [what Vocora should measure as learning](/research-practice/notes/vocora-learning-metrics).

## The open research question

**During dictation after repeated language practice, can reducing conscious, letter-by-letter monitoring of spelling improve *measured* accuracy compared with deliberately monitoring each word—and if such a difference exists, is it attributable to task familiarity, automatic access to written word forms, attention allocation, or some other factor?**
