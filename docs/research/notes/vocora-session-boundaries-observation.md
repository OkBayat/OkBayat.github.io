# The Same Practice, Smaller Finish Lines: An Early Observation from Vocora
{: .no_toc }

{ A first-person note on completing a portion of an unchanged daily workload | fs-6 }

{: .note-title }
> **Early observation · one learner**
>
> I am both the developer of [Vocora](/work/projects/vocora) and the learner reporting this experience. This retrospective account concerns how practice felt after I made session size configurable. It contains no controlled comparison, measured completion-rate improvement, or assessment of learning gains.

My daily practice could involve **200–300 slides**, and sometimes around **400**. When all of that work appeared in one long session, I often found it tiring. Even with a finite amount of material, I did not have a comfortable sense of when the current stretch of work would end.

I changed Vocora so that the learner could choose how many slides to see in each session. For my own practice, I selected **50**. Reaching the end of those 50 slides gave me a clear feeling that I had finished a meaningful part of the work. There could still be more sessions to complete that day, but each one now had its own finish line.

**In my account, I kept the same daily workload and divided it into smaller, learner-selected sessions.** I was not describing a reduction from 300 slides a day to 50.

## What changed in the practice

Previously, the session extended across the larger body of work. The new configuration let a learner choose a smaller session size, such as 20 or 50 slides, while continuing through the day's material across sessions. Fifty was my preference; it was not a universal setting or a number established by research.

The following example makes the distinction concrete:

| Organization of an illustrative 300-slide day | Slides in each session | Sessions needed for the same total |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| One continuous session | 300 | 1 |
| Sessions of 50 slides | 50 | 6 |
| Sessions of 20 slides | 20 | 15 |

This table illustrates the configuration, rather than reporting three conditions I tested. I did not compare 20 and 50 systematically. Nor does it establish an identical number of slides, minutes, or retrieval attempts on every day. My clarification is that the daily workload was not deliberately reduced when I divided it into sessions.

A slide is also a unit in the interface, not a fixed amount of mental effort. Different questions can take different amounts of time and attention. Session size tells the learner how many slides are in that portion of practice; it does not promise a fixed duration or difficulty.

## The feeling of finishing something

What stood out was the sense of completion. Previously, an individual answer was a small step inside a long stretch of unfinished work. With a 50-slide boundary, I could recognize a whole portion as done. That felt satisfying and gave me a sense of success.

The remaining work did not disappear. Completing a session meant that one portion was finished, while the larger daily task could still be open. That distinction made the experience meaningful to me: I could acknowledge progress without having to wait until every slide for the day was complete.

The practice also felt more manageable than the earlier long session. This is a description of my experience, not a score from a fatigue scale. I did not measure whether I worked faster, sustained attention longer, or returned to the next session more reliably.

I have no physiological measurements that could explain this feeling biologically.

## Session boundaries and learner choice

Two features changed together. The immediate task had a nearer endpoint, and I could choose where that endpoint would be. Either could matter to how the experience felt.

One possible interpretation is that 50 slides gave me a goal I could hold in mind more comfortably than several hundred. Another is that selecting the size made the organization fit my own preference. I have not compared a self-selected size with the same size assigned by the application, so I cannot separate these explanations.

This is also a particular kind of division. It organizes practice into sessions; it does not show that the underlying language material became easier or that I formed new mental chunks. That differs from the recognition of word groups described in my [grammar-assembly note](/research-practice/notes/vocora-grammar-automaticity-observation).

Likewise, several sessions do not by themselves establish more widely spaced practice. They might follow one another closely or occur at different times. I have not reconstructed the intervals or breaks. A change in spacing would need its own evidence.

## What published research helps me ask

[Bandura and Schunk (1981)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.41.3.586) compared proximal subgoals, more distant goals, and no goals in children's arithmetic learning. The proximal-goal condition supported learning, perceived efficacy, and interest in that setting. This offers a reason to investigate whether a nearer endpoint helps a learner experience a large task as manageable. The population, task, and measures differ from my adult language practice, and the study does not validate a particular slide count.

[Patall, Cooper, and Wynn (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019545) studied choice among homework assignments in classrooms. Students reported higher intrinsic motivation and perceived competence when they had choices, and their unit-test performance was better in that condition. Choosing an assignment differs from choosing a session boundary, but the study helps identify choice as a separate question worth examining. My observation does not establish that autonomy explains the experience.

[Huang, Jin, and Zhang (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2017.05.001) provide an important qualification. Across laboratory and field tasks, subgoals supported motivation earlier in goal pursuit, whereas focusing on the overall goal became more helpful nearer completion. Their findings do not support a general rule that ever-smaller portions are always better. They suggest examining how a session endpoint relates to the remaining daily goal.

These sources give the observation a research context. None tested this Vocora configuration or measured the experience I report.

## Alternative explanations and limits

I designed the feature and wanted it to help. That expectation could influence what I noticed and remembered. The novelty of the interface change, growing familiarity with the material, the mix of exercises, and other changes in practice could also affect the experience.

The equality of daily workload is part of my retrospective account, not a result established from matched session logs. There is no fixed observation window or systematic series of ratings in this note. I cannot estimate how large the change was or how long it persisted.

A feeling of success after 50 slides is also different from evidence that a learner finishes all remaining sessions. A boundary might encourage continuation or provide a convenient place to stop. I have not measured those possibilities. Similarly, finishing slides says little by itself about later recall or the ability to use English outside the exercise.

The [other Vocora notes](/work/projects/vocora/publications) concern related aspects of my ongoing practice. Their periods may overlap, and this account should not be counted as an independent replication of any earlier finding. It focuses on the organization of a session, not the effects of the review schedule, exercise variety, or feedback.

## A focused next question

**With the same planned daily workload, how do learner-selected session boundaries affect perceived manageability, satisfaction with practice, and continuation through the remaining work?**

A prospective comparison could examine one long session, assigned smaller sessions, and learner-selected smaller sessions. The assigned and selected conditions would need comparable session sizes to distinguish the effect of choice from the size itself. The planned material, difficulty, exercise mix, and feedback would need to be comparable; the amount actually completed would remain an outcome.

Brief ratings at common points in the daily workload, as well as at session boundaries, could capture the experience. Records of completed slides, return to the next session, time spent, and actual breaks would show what happened across the whole day. Analysis should include unfinished days, rather than only learners who completed every session. Any question about learning would require a separate delayed assessment.

For now, the observation is that the same daily work felt different when I could complete it in portions I had chosen. The satisfaction of finishing each portion is clear in my recollection. Whether that experience supports sustained practice or better learning remains open.
