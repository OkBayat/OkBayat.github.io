# From Knowing Grammar to Using It Naturally: An Early Observation from Vocora
{: .no_toc }

{ A first-person account of two turning points in grammar use and listening | fs-6 }

{: .note-title }
> **Early observation · one learner, with informal feedback**
>
> I am both the developer of [Vocora](/work/projects/vocora) and the learner reporting this experience. The account is retrospective. Teacher and peer comments are informal reports, and I collected no standardized speaking or listening scores.

For a long time, I could understand a grammar explanation without being able to use the structure comfortably in conversation. After reorganizing my grammar practice into smaller targets and reviewing **once a day**, I noticed a change in a speaking lesson **within approximately two weeks**. Around **another month and a half later**, using and recognizing familiar structures felt more established, including when I listened to film dialogue.

The important change for me was access: grammar I understood during study seemed more available while I was trying to communicate or follow someone else's meaning.

## From understanding an explanation to practicing its parts

I had studied *Cambridge Grammar for IELTS*, by Diana Hopkins and Pauline Cullen. I could follow the explanations and examples, but speaking demanded something different. I had to express an idea, listen to another person, and respond without stopping to work through a rule.

Forms such as the present perfect, present perfect continuous, past perfect, and past perfect continuous were familiar from study, yet I rarely reached for them spontaneously. I often hesitated, confused structures, or returned to simpler sentences. This is my recollection of how speaking felt, rather than a measured count of which forms I used.

I began organizing broad topics into **skills** and then smaller **micro-skills**. The second conditional illustrates the design idea:

> If I had more time, I would practice English every day.

The difficulty might involve the past-form verb in the if-clause, **“would” + base-form verb** in the result clause, connecting the clauses, or understanding the hypothetical meaning. Naming the whole topic does not identify which part is difficult. Breaking it down gave me more specific practice targets.

This example explains the approach; it is not a transcript of an exercise. The boundaries between micro-skills were design choices. I practiced these targets through a **Leitner-style review session once a day**, before I had completed every construction.

## The first turning point: a speaking lesson

Within approximately two weeks of starting this routine, I found myself using forms in a speaking lesson that had previously been difficult to access. Perfect and continuous structures seemed to appear while I was concentrating on what I wanted to say. I was not deliberately naming a tense before each sentence.

My teacher also remarked on a difference. As I remember it, he asked what had changed since our previous session because I seemed to be using grammar more comfortably. That is a paraphrase of a remembered conversation. I did not record the lesson or collect a standardized teacher rating.

The change felt striking because it concerned familiar knowledge becoming usable. It was not simply a wish to produce longer sentences. A form such as **“I had finished before the meeting”** expresses a particular time relationship; its value depends on whether that relationship fits what I mean.

Without recordings, I cannot establish how often I used the target forms, how accurate each use was, or whether my overall speaking fluency changed. What I can report is that previously underused structures felt more accessible during a real conversation, and my teacher noticed a difference too.

## A later change in listening

Around another month and a half after that first turning point, the change felt more established. I sometimes used a familiar structure and only afterward noticed which one I had used. Grammar felt less like a separate set of explanations I had to retrieve deliberately.

During this broader period, returning to an English-language film I already knew was particularly memorable. Previously, I could follow much of what was happening. On this viewing, I felt that I understood more of what the characters meant and felt.

I was not stopping to label every grammatical construction. I seemed to follow the dialogue more directly. The difference between understanding the events and sensing more of a speaker's intention or emotion mattered to me.

That experience does not isolate grammar. Vocabulary, delivery, acting, context, and remembering the film could all have contributed. It is a report of richer perceived understanding, not a measured improvement in listening or proof that grammar practice caused it.

My timeline is approximate:

| Point in the experience | What I reported |
| --- | --- |
| Before the new routine | Grammar explanations made sense during study, but varied forms were difficult to use in conversation. |
| Practice routine | Topics were divided into skills and micro-skills, with one Leitner-style grammar-review session a day. |
| Within approximately two weeks | I noticed more spontaneous use during a speaking lesson; my teacher also commented on a difference. |
| Around another month and a half later | Familiar grammar felt more readily available; this later period also included richer perceived understanding of familiar film dialogue. |

These are remembered stages, not exact start and end dates. I continued speaking lessons, films, and other contact with English, even though another dedicated grammar resource was not my main practice method during this period.

## What other learners said

A few friends who had used the practice approach described similar experiences in informal conversations. I did not record a fixed sample, ask standardized questions, or independently assess their performance. Their comments helped motivate further investigation, but do not establish replication or an effect across learners.

## How research helps frame the question

[DeKeyser (1997)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263197002040) studied the development of grammatical skills after explicit learning and found that practice effects were strongly specific to the skill practiced. This makes the difference between solving an exercise and using grammar in conversation a central question. It does not establish that the Vocora routine caused my experience.

[Kim and Webb's meta-analysis (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479) supports the general relevance of spaced practice to second-language learning. My routine, however, combined scheduling with smaller practice targets, repetition, feedback, prior knowledge, and continued English exposure. This account cannot identify the contribution of each component or validate a particular Leitner schedule.

I also need to distinguish a feeling of less conscious effort from a demonstrated change in the kind of knowledge I possess. [Schmidt (1990)](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/11.2.129) discusses attention, noticing, and implicit learning as distinct questions. My recollection of not naming rules cannot settle them.

## What would make the observation more informative

A useful next step would collect speaking and listening measures before a defined practice period and again afterward. Recorded responses to unfamiliar speaking prompts could be assessed by independent raters for the accuracy and appropriateness of target structures. New listening clips could separate literal understanding from interpretation of speaker intention, avoiding the familiarity of a rewatched film.

Practice records would need to show the targets, exercises, session duration, and review schedule. A comparison using a similar amount of practice would help distinguish the effects of smaller targets from scheduling and general exposure. Delayed assessment would show whether any measured change persists.

For now, the account supports a narrower question: **Can focused grammar practice help a learner access familiar structures during conversation, and what evidence would distinguish that change from general practice, exposure, and expectation?**
