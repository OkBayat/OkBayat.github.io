# From Knowing Grammar to Using It Naturally: An Early Observation from Vocora
{: .no_toc }

{ A first-person account of two turning points in grammar use and listening | fs-6 }

{: .note-title }
> **Early observation — not a scientific finding**
>
> I am documenting my own experience as an English learner and the developer of [Vocora](/work/projects/vocora). I noticed a striking change after breaking grammar into skills and micro-skills and practicing with a Leitner-style review system **once a day**. The first noticeable shift appeared **within approximately two weeks**; around **another month and a half later**, my sense of ease and recognition seemed more established. My speaking teacher also remarked on a change, and a few friends informally described similar experiences. These are retrospective observations, not independent assessments, measured improvements, or evidence of causation. I have no controlled comparison, pre/post speaking scores, or validated test of implicit learning.

For a long time, I could understand a grammar lesson and still fail to use what I had learned when it mattered.

I studied with *[Cambridge Grammar for IELTS](https://shop.cambridge.org/english/product/2700215337)*, by Diana Hopkins and Pauline Cullen. The book's explanations made sense to me as I read them. I could follow the examples and understand the rules. But when I tried to speak English, that understanding did not feel readily available. I would hesitate, confuse constructions, and fall back on simple sentences.

It was a frustrating distinction: **I knew what the grammar rule meant when I was studying it; I did not know how to retrieve and use the right structure while having a conversation.**

I eventually stopped treating an entire grammatical topic as one learning target. I tried organizing each topic as a **skill**, identifying smaller **micro-skills** within it, and practicing those targets through repeated, spaced review in Vocora.

What followed felt like a breakthrough. I want to record exactly what I noticed, what other people said to me, what I might be interpreting, and what remains unproven.

## Before Vocora: knowing a rule was not the same as being able to use it

Reading grammar explanations was not the difficult part. I often understood them completely **at the time of reading**.

The difficulty appeared later, especially in speaking. When a conversation moved quickly, I had to respond to a person, express an idea, listen for a reply, and decide what to say next. Recalling the correct grammar rule at the same moment felt different from recognizing that rule in a book.

I sometimes mixed up structures or simply did not reach for them. For example, forms such as the **present perfect**, **present perfect continuous**, **past perfect**, and **past perfect continuous** were familiar from study, yet I rarely used them in my everyday speech. My sentences tended to remain simple.

The gap was not necessarily that I *had never learned* the rules. It was that the relevant knowledge did not feel available **at the speed of conversation**.

I cannot establish from memory how frequently I used each tense before this experience. The contrast above describes the way my speaking felt to me, not a counted inventory of grammatical forms.

## The change in design: from topics, to skills, to micro-skills

My first attempt was to turn broad grammar topics into skills.

That helped me ask a more useful question: instead of saying *“I have a problem with the second conditional,”* could I identify **which operation** I was having trouble with?

A second conditional provides a simple illustration. In a sentence like:

> **If I had more time, I would practice English every day.**

I could be struggling with the **if-clause** (*if + subject + past-form verb*), the **result clause** (*would + base-form verb*), the relationship between the clauses, or the intended hypothetical meaning.

Knowing the label **second conditional** does not tell me which part is failing. Dividing the larger skill into micro-skills made it possible, at least in my design, to focus practice more narrowly.

The illustration above is a grammatical example of the design idea, **not a verbatim record of a particular Vocora exercise**. Micro-skill boundaries are instructional design decisions; their effectiveness still needs independent assessment.

I then put these practice targets into a **Leitner-style review routine**. My personal schedule was **one grammar-review session a day**, with the material revisited over time. I had not completed or mastered every grammatical construction before noticing the first change.

This is important: I am describing the whole arrangement—focused micro-skill tasks, repeated practice, review, and continued contact with English—not showing that any individual component caused the result.

## The first turning point: a speaking lesson less than two weeks later

Within roughly **two weeks** of beginning the new practice routine, I experienced something I did not expect.

During a speaking session with my teacher, I found myself using grammatical forms that I had almost never used spontaneously before. More complex perfect and continuous structures seemed to appear naturally while I was trying to communicate. I was not deliberately running through a checklist of tenses.

At one point, I became aware of what I was doing. I was speaking and using structures that had previously felt difficult to access. The striking part was that I was **not consciously choosing a tense name before each sentence**.

My teacher also drew attention to a difference. As I recall the exchange, he asked what had changed since our previous session, because I was using grammar more comfortably than before. I remember being surprised that he had noticed it independently.

That is a **paraphrase of a remembered conversation**, not a recorded quotation or a standardized assessment. I did not collect an audio sample or a teacher rating that could quantify the change.

### What “using more grammar” felt like

Consider these *illustrative* contrasts—not transcripts of what I said in that lesson:

| A simpler way to express an idea | A structure I had previously found hard to use spontaneously |
| --- | --- |
| “I work on this project.” | “I **have been working** on this project for two weeks.” |
| “I finished it before the meeting.” | “I **had finished** it before the meeting.” |
| “I waited for a long time before you arrived.” | “I **had been waiting** for a long time before you arrived.” |

The point is not that longer or more complex grammar is automatically better. Each sentence expresses a **different time relationship or meaning**. The observation is that structures I previously knew mainly as explanations began to feel more accessible when the conversation called for them.

I cannot show, without recordings, that my rate of using those structures increased or that each use was accurate and contextually appropriate. The experience was vivid; the evidence remains limited.

## What a few other learners told me

Because the shift surprised me, I spoke informally with **a few friends who had also used the practice approach**. They told me they had experienced something similar: forms that had been difficult to use deliberately seemed easier to recognize or use after practice.

These conversations mattered to me as a reason to investigate the idea further, but **they are not a study sample**. I did not record a fixed number of respondents, obtain a standardized baseline, define identical questions, or verify their outcomes independently. People who choose to discuss an encouraging experience are not a representative sample.

I therefore treat their responses as **informal learner reports**, not replication, independent confirmation, or proof that a given routine works for others.

## A different surprise: returning to a familiar English-language film

The speaking lesson was not the only moment that caught my attention.

I was also noticing a change while listening to English in films and music. I decided to return to an English-language film I already knew and liked. I had watched it before.

Previously, I could often follow the events—understand broadly **what was happening**. This time, I felt that I was hearing **more of what the characters meant and felt**. A line of dialogue seemed to carry an emotional or interpersonal significance that I had not appreciated so readily before.

That distinction matters to me. **Understanding the plot** and **sensing the speaker's stance, intention, or emotional implication** are not identical experiences.

A grammatical construction can contribute to meaning without the viewer explicitly labeling it. A speaker might say:

> “I **have been waiting** for you.”

Rather than merely reporting that waiting occurred, this wording can, depending on delivery and context, frame a duration extending toward the present and carry frustration, expectation, or relief.

This is an **invented example to illustrate the distinction**, not a line transcribed from the film I rewatched. The emotional interpretation would also depend on vocabulary, tone of voice, acting, context, and prior familiarity—not grammar alone.

In my experience, I was not stopping the film and thinking, *“That was the present perfect continuous; therefore the speaker means X.”* I felt that I was following the meaning more directly, with less conscious naming and analysis of forms. I remember it as though a door had opened.

That is my **subjective description**. I cannot verify that I literally understood everything, nor that the newly felt nuance came from grammar practice rather than remembering the film, growing familiarity with English, increased attention to dialogue, or other aspects of the performance.

## The second turning point: approximately another month and a half later

The early speaking change was noticeable within approximately two weeks. **About another month and a half after that first turning point**, I felt the change becoming more established.

Grammar no longer seemed like a separate set of rules I had to fetch from a textbook each time. I could recognize many familiar structures and their likely meaning more readily. Sometimes I used a structure and only afterward noticed what I had used.

The best metaphor I have for that feeling is that grammar had become **part of me**, almost like a movement I did not have to plan step by step.

This second stage is a later personal impression, not a measured retention result. I did not systematically log every daily session, pause other exposure to English, or test the same previously unseen material at fixed intervals. Although I was not using another dedicated grammar-learning resource as my main practice method during this period, I continued living with language through speaking lessons, films, and other encounters. Those experiences could also have contributed.

The time sequence is an **approximate retrospective timeline**, not a tightly documented intervention period:

| Stage | My account |
| --- | --- |
| Before the redesign | I understood grammar while reading *Cambridge Grammar for IELTS*, but struggled to use varied forms in live speaking. |
| Change in practice | Grammar topics were decomposed into skills and micro-skills, then practiced with a once-daily Leitner-style review routine. |
| Within approximately two weeks | I noticed more spontaneous use of previously underused structures during a speaking session; my teacher also remarked on the difference. |
| Around another month and a half later | I felt more ready access to grammar and meaning, including a different experience of listening to a familiar film. |

## What this observation does and does not suggest

Several distinctions help me avoid claiming more than I know.

### 1. Knowing grammar and using grammar are different outcomes

Recognizing a rule in a book is not the same task as producing a suitable sentence under conversational time pressure. Work in second-language skill acquisition has investigated how practice relates to the development of efficient grammatical performance. That literature gives me a way to formulate questions; it does **not** demonstrate that micro-skills or Vocora produced my experience.

### 2. Less conscious rule naming does not prove implicit learning

I experienced less conscious analysis while speaking and listening. That does **not** prove that I acquired implicit knowledge or that my brain was processing language without awareness. A familiar explicit skill may become more fluent; an accessible phrase may be recalled as a whole; attention may simply have shifted toward meaning. Distinguishing these explanations requires evidence beyond how the moment felt.

### 3. More sensitivity to a character's feeling is not a grammar-only outcome

A film communicates through grammar, vocabulary, sound, intonation, timing, gesture, acting, and narrative context. I might have noticed more emotional significance because of any combination of these. Rewatching a film also supplies prior knowledge of the plot. My account cannot isolate grammatical comprehension from broader listening and pragmatic interpretation.

### 4. Practice and spacing are related but separable hypotheses

A once-daily routine contained multiple possible contributors: breaking a broad objective into smaller tasks, recognizing errors, repeated retrieval, spaced scheduling, existing knowledge, and continued exposure. Published research on spacing supports the **general relevance** of distribution across learning sessions, but it does not establish that my exact schedule caused a two-week speaking change.

### 5. This observation concerns grammar—not yet every skill in Vocora

The experience strongly influenced my **design decision** to apply micro-skill thinking more broadly to reading, writing, listening, and speaking activities in Vocora.

That is a design direction, **not** a finding about those skills. I have **not yet observed or documented comparable, sufficiently clear outcomes** across those other areas, and I do not want to imply that I have.

I have documented related but different experiences:

This note is specifically about a **perceived transfer from grammar study to spontaneous conversation**, and a **subjectively richer interpretation of film dialogue**. None of the three notes, individually or combined, constitutes evidence of an experimentally verified learning effect.

## What I would need to study next

The experience is worth examining precisely because I do not know which part of it was responsible for the difference.

A future investigation could:

1. **Define the practice precisely.** Record the grammar micro-skills, exercise types, items encountered, review schedule, and amount of daily practice instead of relying on a general label such as “micro-learning.”
2. **Measure a baseline before training.** Collect recorded speaking responses to comparable prompts and test comprehension of unfamiliar dialogue, not just familiar movie scenes.
3. **Compare practice conditions.** Where feasible, compare the same amount of practice delivered through conventional topic-level exercises, micro-skill-focused practice, and appropriate scheduling conditions, rather than confounding all components.
4. **Assess real conversational use.** Have independent raters, blind to the practice condition, evaluate the *accuracy and appropriateness* of target forms, not merely how many complex tenses appear.
5. **Test listening and pragmatic understanding separately.** Use new clips and questions that distinguish plot recall, literal sentence meaning, and interpretation of a character's attitude or intention.
6. **Measure retention and transfer.** Repeat testing after a delay and include unfamiliar prompts; distinguish improvements on practiced exercise formats from independent speaking and listening.
7. **Collect learner and teacher reports without treating them as scores.** Record testimonials transparently, obtain consent, and keep them separate from objective task performance.

If later data corroborate some part of this experience, it would still be necessary to examine alternative explanations, including motivation, teacher influence, general English exposure, familiarity with testing, and expectation effects.

## Research background

These sources provide context for the questions; **none studied this specific Vocora observation or showed that the practice caused my reported changes**.

- Hopkins, D., & Cullen, P. *[Cambridge Grammar for IELTS](https://shop.cambridge.org/english/product/2700215337)*, Cambridge University Press. The publisher identifies the later title *IELTS Grammar for Bands 6.5 and Above* as its successor; it is the earlier Cambridge book I remember studying.
- DeKeyser, R. M. (1997). [Beyond Explicit Rule Learning: Automatizing Second Language Morphosyntax](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263197002040). *Studies in Second Language Acquisition*, 19(2), 195–221. A study of practice and increasingly efficient grammatical performance; transfer across tasks remains an empirical question.
- Kim, S. K., & Webb, S. (2022). [The Effects of Spaced Practice on Second Language Learning: A Meta-Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479). *Language Learning*, 72(1), 269–319. Evidence about spaced second-language learning generally, **not** evidence for a particular Leitner schedule or this account.
- Schmidt, R. W. (1990). [The Role of Consciousness in Second Language Learning](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/11.2.129). *Applied Linguistics*, 11(2), 129–158. A reason to distinguish conscious attention, noticing, and hypotheses about implicit learning.

## The question I want to investigate

**Can targeted grammar micro-skill practice with repeated, spaced review help learners move from understanding grammatical rules during study to using them appropriately without deliberate rule-by-rule analysis in spontaneous speaking—and, if so, does that learning also transfer to a richer understanding of meaning and speaker intention in unfamiliar authentic listening, beyond the effects of general exposure, familiarity, and expectation?**
