# When a Mistake Became a Question: An Early Observation of Metacognitive Reflection in Vocora
{: .no_toc }

{ A first-person field note on noticing how I learn, not just whether I answer correctly | fs-6 }

{: .note-title }
> **Early observation — not a demonstrated learning effect**
>
> This note expands a one-page practice account I shared privately with Professor Rosemary Reilly on **16 November 2026**. After reading her 2005 work and Reilly and Bramwell's 2007 paper on reflection and metacognition, I adapted one aspect of those group-based ideas into a brief, individual feature in [Vocora](/work/projects/vocora), the English-learning platform I am developing. Over approximately **two weeks**, I noticed a change in my own attention to mistakes. I also described a shift in my approximate daily accuracy ranges, from **65–72%** in a preceding period of roughly five months to **78–82%** during the two weeks. These are descriptive summaries of recorded correct/incorrect responses, **not calculated group means, a controlled comparison, or proof that the feature caused the change**. My practice volume was broadly similar but **not identical** across the periods, and task difficulty was not matched. A family member shared a similar subjective experience, but her outcomes have not been analyzed.

I built something small in Vocora: after a learner makes two consecutive incorrect responses in a practice session, the exercise briefly stops and asks two questions. Not more grammar instruction. Not an immediate stream of extra exercises. Just an invitation to look at the mistake and decide what to do differently.

I expected that the questions might encourage reflection. What surprised me was the kind of mistake I began to notice in myself.

Initially, the incorrect answers themselves were what I noticed. Then another pattern came into focus: **sometimes I rushed precisely because I thought I already knew the answer**.

That discovery, rather than an accuracy percentage, is the reason I want to preserve this early observation.

## The problem I was trying to make visible

An incorrect answer tells a learner that something did not work. It does not necessarily tell them **what kind of process led to the error**.

A spelling mistake might mean that I did not know the word's written form. It might also mean that I misheard it, overlooked its meaning, typed too quickly, or failed to check a familiar irregular form. A grammar error might reveal a gap in knowledge, confusion about a construction, or simply insufficient attention at that moment.

The difference matters for the next attempt. If I always react to an incorrect answer by doing more of the same practice, I may never examine whether the problem was knowledge, attention, strategy, or the relationship between them.

I wanted to know whether a small pause **inside the learning flow** could help me make those distinctions for myself.

## Where the idea came from: group reflection and metacognition

Two papers by Rosemary C. Reilly shaped the question.

In **Reilly (2005)**, public reflection is considered in the context of workplace learning and the development of expert thinking. People step back from actions and examine what happened, including the assumptions behind their decisions. In **Reilly and Bramwell (2007)**, public reflection circles are explored in relation to metacognition and good practice. I was particularly interested in the attention given to reviewing one's actions, considering alternatives, and monitoring one's own approach.

These studies concern **public, socially situated reflection**. The participation of other people and the shared meaning-making process are not incidental details.

What I built is **not a reproduction of their research**. I took an inspired but much narrower question into a different setting:

**Could a very brief, private moment of reflection help an individual learner recognize a pattern in their own mistakes and change their next action?**

No group discussion is present in the Vocora interaction. Two multiple-choice answers cannot be assumed to provide everything that a public reflection circle makes possible. Their value, if any, needs to be studied on its own terms.

## What I implemented in Vocora

The version described in my 16 November practice note used a straightforward flow.

When **two incorrect answers occur consecutively for the first time in a practice session**, Vocora presents two multiple-choice reflection slides:

1. **Why do you think you made this mistake?**
2. **What will you do differently next time?**

The learner selects responses and then returns to normal practice. The same pair of slides **does not reappear in that session**.

The interaction deliberately separates looking backward from looking forward: first consider a possible explanation, then choose a next-step strategy. It does not require a free-text essay or a long detour away from practice.

I chose the two-error trigger and the once-per-session limit as **product-design decisions**, not because Reilly's studies or any other publication established these exact thresholds. A later paper on digital reflection-prompt design by **Smith and colleagues (2025)** helped frame the concern that prompts can interrupt a learner's workflow and impose effort. That research does not validate the specific Vocora trigger or demonstrate that these two slides improve performance.

The detail about timing is worth emphasizing. If reflection interrupts too often, it could become another burden. If it appears only after practice has ended, it might no longer connect to the decision the learner just made. I tried one bounded design; I have not established its optimal timing.

## Two weeks of use: the observation I did not expect

My sister and I both tried this experience over approximately two weeks. We described the slides as a welcome moment to pause during a succession of questions.

For me, the reflection sometimes continued after the slides disappeared.

When I selected a reason for an error and considered what to do next, I became more attentive to the very next responses. I remember **rushing less**, paying closer attention to **irregular verb forms**, and sometimes retrieving a word's **meaning before working through its spelling**.

None of those is an independently measured behavioral variable in this note. They are actions I **noticed myself taking**.

### The surprising part: familiar questions could make me careless

I began to recognize a difference in how I approached unfamiliar and familiar material.

With something new, I often read carefully because I knew I needed to work it out. With something familiar, I could decide too quickly that I already knew it. I would move faster, pay less attention, and sometimes make the very errors I thought I had outgrown.

The reflective prompt seemed to make that habit easier for me to notice.

For example, the distinction can be expressed as an **illustrative reconstruction**, not as a recorded Vocora item:

| Without examining the decision | With a moment of self-monitoring |
| --- | --- |
| “I know this word. I'll answer quickly.” | “I recognize the word, but have I checked the exact spelling?” |
| “This grammar pattern is easy.” | “Am I using the right verb form, or am I responding from habit?” |
| “I got it wrong; I need another question.” | “Was the error caused by a gap in knowledge or by rushing?” |

I do **not** claim to have demonstrated why every mistake happened. A selected answer to a reflection prompt is a learner's hypothesis, not a diagnosis. But I found that **forming a hypothesis about my mistake** could change the way I approached the next one.

This is the observation that matters most to me: I was learning to pay attention not only to the answer, but to **the process by which I reached it**.

### A second personal report

My sister reported a similar overall experience of finding the pause useful. I have **not** analyzed her responses or learning outcomes, and I do not treat her account as a second experimental participant with verified improvement.

We were also not independent of the design. I developed the feature and had expectations about its potential; we were both aware of why it was there. Those facts matter when interpreting what we felt.

## The accuracy numbers I noticed

I have recorded correct and incorrect practice answers over time. In the one-page note I shared on 16 November, I described the following **approximate daily accuracy ranges**:

| Period | Approximate daily accuracy described in my note |
| --- | --- |
| Roughly five preceding months | **65–72%**, typically |
| Approximately two weeks using the reflection feature | **78–82%** |

Previously, days above 80% had been unusual for me and often involved unusually extensive practice.

These are **ranges from my retrospective summary of daily recorded correct/incorrect counts**. They are not statistically calculated before-and-after means, not a comparison between equivalent sets of exercises, and not an independent analysis of the underlying records.

An important qualification from my subsequent review: **the daily volumes of practice were broadly comparable, but not exactly the same**. I also did not hold constant the difficulty or mix of questions, the amount of repeated exposure, or changes in my familiarity with Vocora. It would therefore be misleading to present the two ranges as a measured percentage-point effect of reflection.

The numbers made me curious. They do not resolve why my performance differed.

Indeed, the deeper observation could remain worth investigating even if a later controlled analysis found **no overall improvement in accuracy**. Becoming able to describe how I make a mistake is a different potential outcome from getting more answers correct.

## Awareness is not the same as an explanation

I can separate three layers of this experience.

**What was recorded:** Vocora recorded correct and incorrect attempts over the broader period. The reported daily accuracy ranges were summarized from those records. Logging of responses to the reflection questions **began only recently**, so I cannot reconstruct a complete historical record linking a selected reason or plan to subsequent responses.

**What I experienced:** After seeing and answering the two prompts, I sometimes paid closer attention to my own approach, especially the tendency to rush through material I thought I already knew. My sister also described the pause as useful in a general sense, but her performance was not assessed.

**What I hypothesize:** A reason-and-next-step prompt might encourage self-monitoring that carries forward into practice. However, the same behavioral shift might also be caused by simply interrupting an automatic sequence of responses, allowing a short rest, or drawing attention to the most recent mistake.

These explanations are not interchangeable. The present account cannot determine which is correct.

## What this early observation cannot establish

There are important limitations.

- **No controlled comparison.** I did not randomly assign practice sessions to reflection prompts, a neutral pause, and unchanged practice.
- **Unequal and unstandardized practice.** My workload was approximately similar across periods, not identical; the content and difficulty of the exercises were not held constant.
- **Different observation windows.** Roughly five months of previous activity and two weeks of new activity do not provide a balanced comparison by themselves.
- **Approximate reporting.** The ranges are descriptive recollections of recorded counts, not independently calculated effects, confidence intervals, or evidence of a statistically reliable change.
- **Developer expectation.** I built the feature and was interested in the outcome. That can influence both my attention and my interpretation.
- **Brief, recent reflection logging.** I cannot yet connect a learner's chosen explanation and plan to an adequate series of subsequent attempts.
- **No independent outcome for my sister.** Her response is an informal experience report, not an evaluated result.
- **Other possible causes.** Practice volume, item familiarity, feedback, task difficulty, exposure, motivation, and the neutral effect of pausing could all contribute.

Most importantly, **a learner noticing a habit does not prove that the habit has changed durably**. The observation covers a short period in one learning setting. I have not established whether it persists without prompts or transfers to unfamiliar tasks or everyday language use.

## What I would study next

My next question is more specific than whether reflection “works.”

I would want to distinguish **the content of the reflection** from **the fact of being interrupted**. A small prospective study could compare three conditions: usual uninterrupted practice; a short, duration-matched neutral pause; and the two-question reason-and-plan reflection used in Vocora.

The study should keep the following elements explicit:

1. **Comparable learning opportunities.** Balance exercise types, item difficulty, exposure, and time or number of responses per condition.
2. **Recorded timing.** Log when errors occur, when a prompt or neutral pause appears, how long the interruption takes, and when practice resumes.
3. **Separate behavioral and performance outcomes.** Analyze accuracy and error types, but also whether learners later slow down appropriately, verify a suspected irregular form, or change the strategy they previously selected.
4. **Reflection answers as hypotheses.** Keep selected reasons and intended plans with consent, without assuming the choices reveal a true unconscious cause of an error.
5. **Delayed and unprompted tests.** Check whether any self-monitoring behavior persists after the prompts disappear and on new exercises.
6. **Sufficient participants and transparent analysis.** Avoid inferring population-wide benefits from my own two-week experience.

A matched neutral pause matters because I enjoyed the interruption itself. If a neutral pause produced the same effects, the specific two-question format might not be necessary. If the reflective version led to additional durable self-monitoring beyond that pause, there would be a more precise phenomenon to study.

This question also connects to the concern raised in other reflective-practice work: **does a change remain available when the support that prompted it is removed?** I cannot answer that yet.

## The question I want to investigate

**Can brief, private questions about why a learner made a mistake and what they plan to do differently improve self-monitoring during subsequent practice beyond the effect of a matched neutral pause—and, if so, do any changes persist across sessions and transfer to unfamiliar tasks when those prompts are no longer present?**
