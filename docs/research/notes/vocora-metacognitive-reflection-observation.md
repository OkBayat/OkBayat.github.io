# When a Mistake Became a Question: An Early Observation of Metacognitive Reflection in Vocora
{: .no_toc }

{ A two-week field note on becoming more aware of how I learn | fs-6 }

{: .note-title }
> **Early observation · personal account with informal feedback**
>
> I am both the developer of [Vocora](/work/projects/vocora) and the learner reporting this experience. This account combines my own observations, approximate summaries of my recorded practice accuracy, and informal feedback from a family member. It does not establish that reflection prompts caused a learning improvement.

After adding two short reflection questions to Vocora, I noticed a habit I had previously overlooked: **I sometimes rushed because I thought I already knew the answer**.

New material usually made me careful. Familiar questions could have the opposite effect. When I recognized a word or grammar pattern, I sometimes answered before checking it properly. During the pauses after mistakes, I began to notice that difference in my own approach.

Over approximately two weeks, I found myself rushing less, checking irregular verb forms more carefully, and recalling a word's meaning before working through its spelling. I also noticed higher daily accuracy ranges during this period. The experience raised two related questions: was I becoming better at monitoring my approach, and what explained the change in my answers?

## How the reflection worked

In the version I used, the **first occurrence of two consecutive incorrect answers in a practice session** triggered two multiple-choice slides:

1. **Why do you think you made this mistake?**
2. **What will you do differently next time?**

After selecting responses, the learner returned to practice. The pair did **not appear again in that session**.

The first question invited a possible explanation; the second invited a plan for the next attempt. I chose the two-error trigger and the once-per-session limit to keep the interaction brief. Those thresholds were design choices, not settings established as effective by research.

An answer to the first question is also a learner's interpretation. A selected reason does not by itself establish the cause of an error. The useful question for me was whether considering a reason and a next step changed how I approached subsequent practice.

## What I noticed

My sister and I both experienced the slides as a welcome pause during continuous questions, almost like taking a breath. Her feedback concerned the overall experience; I have not analyzed her learning outcomes.

For me, choosing a response seemed to carry forward after the slides disappeared. I noticed myself checking details that I had sometimes passed over. The strongest example was the difference between unfamiliar material, which drew my attention, and familiar material, which could invite a hurried answer.

This did not give me a reliable explanation for every mistake. It did make one recurring possibility more visible: knowing a form and attending to it carefully on a particular attempt are different things. I could recognize something and still answer carelessly.

These descriptions are my recollections of how I practiced. I did not measure checking behavior, independently code error causes, or establish how long any change lasted. They remain useful observations to investigate without treating them as verified mechanisms.

## The accuracy ranges

Alongside that change in awareness, I reported the following approximate daily accuracy ranges from recorded correct and incorrect responses:

| Observation window | My reported daily accuracy |
| --- | --- |
| Roughly five preceding months | Typically **65–72%** |
| Approximately two weeks using the reflection feature | Approximately **78–82%** |

Previously, days above 80% had been rare for me and usually involved unusually extensive practice.

These figures are **approximate summaries of recorded counts, not calculated before-and-after means**. They should not be read as the minimum and maximum of every day, as group results, or as an estimated treatment effect. I have not presented an independent analysis of the underlying daily records here.

My practice volume was **broadly similar, but not identical**, across the periods. Exercise difficulty, the mix of questions, repeated exposure, and familiarity were not controlled. The observation windows also differed substantially in length. Those differences prevent me from attributing the higher ranges to the reflection feature.

Correct and incorrect responses had been recorded throughout. At the time of this observation, logging of reflection choices had only recently begun. I therefore could not link all earlier selected reasons and plans to later performance. Awareness of a habit and higher accuracy occurred in the same period; their relationship remains uncertain.

## The research that informed the design

Reading [Reilly (2005)](https://spectrum.library.concordia.ca/id/eprint/6434/) and [Reilly and Bramwell (2007)](https://ojs.adelaide.edu.au/index.php/EDEQ/article/view/494) led me to consider how examining an action and possible alternatives might support learning. These related qualitative accounts concern public reflection among novice group facilitators. The 2007 paper describes reviewing actions and considering alternatives on page 47, and examines self-monitoring on pages 54–55.

The social setting matters. Participants could hear other perspectives and reconsider their interpretations together. Vocora's private questions do not reproduce that process. I adapted an idea from those accounts to ask a narrower question about individual practice.

[Smith and colleagues (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3757980.3758006) also informed my concern about interruption and effort. Their qualitative study examined students' experiences of different digital reflection-prompt designs and identified difficulties integrating prompts into learning workflows. It supports taking timing and response burden seriously; it does not validate Vocora's trigger, frequency, or learning outcomes.

## What could explain the experience?

One possibility is that choosing a reason and a plan encouraged self-monitoring during the next exercises. Another is that the pause itself interrupted a run of hurried responses. Changes in difficulty, familiarity, practice volume, motivation, or repeated exposure could also have contributed.

I developed the feature and was interested in its outcome, which could shape what I noticed. This informal account has no comparison condition, and the family feedback does not provide an independent performance result. It also cannot establish lasting changes, benefits for other learners, or transfer to new tasks.

My [dictation-attention note](/research-practice/notes/vocora-dictation-attention-observation) describes a different impression: spelling sometimes felt easier when I monitored each decision less. That raises a useful distinction to test between briefly reviewing one's approach after an error and consciously checking every step while answering. These reports do not establish when more or less conscious monitoring helps.

The [other Vocora observations](/work/projects/vocora/publications) also describe aspects of my ongoing learning. Their practice periods may overlap, and I have not reconstructed an exact shared calendar. They should be read as related accounts from the same learner, rather than independent demonstrations of effectiveness. The daily accuracy ranges here describe a different outcome from the delayed spelling check in the [retention note](/research-practice/notes/vocora-leitner-spelling-retention-observation); the figures cannot be combined into a single improvement estimate.

## The next question

**Do these private questions support self-monitoring beyond the effect of a brief pause?**

A prospective comparison could examine usual practice, a duration-matched neutral pause, and the two-question reflection. It would need comparable exercise opportunities and records of question difficulty, exposure, response timing, and accuracy. It should examine whether the selected plan is followed in later behavior, while treating the selected reason as a hypothesis about the mistake.

Delayed checks and practice without prompts would help show whether any change persists. That would move the question beyond whether I enjoyed the pause or answered more items correctly during a short period.

The observation I want to preserve is simple: after introducing the prompts, I became more aware of how familiarity could change my attention. Whether that awareness leads to reliable, lasting learning gains remains open.
